## Part I — The Plain-English Edition

### What this project is

fabric-of-space is a solar system that lives in a browser. The planets sit where NASA says they actually are, gravity is computed honestly between every pair of bodies, and space itself is drawn as a stretchy grid that sags under mass — the "fabric." The mission, stated plainly this week for the first time: **make gravitational effects visible on screen.** The fabric is the instrument; the galaxy is the destination. One law stands above all others: **the physics never cheats.** Only the picture cheats, to keep things visible, and every visual trick is confessed in a public ledger called CHEATS.md. Eight confessions now, each documented like an incident report.

### What happened this week

Last week's recap ended on a cliffhanger: one clean measurement remained to make Einstein's number appear. This week had five acts, and the first act paid that debt.

**Act 1 — the dime landed.** Mercury's orbit swivels a hair more than Newton allows — 43 arcseconds per century, the width of a dime seen from 85 meters, and Einstein predicted it exactly. Our measurement: run 150 years with Einstein's correction off, run the identical 150 years with it on, subtract. Everything shared cancels; what refuses to cancel is the answer. Two independent witnesses read the result: **+42.8 and +42.9 arcseconds per century**, against Einstein's 42.98. The dime is on the wall.

**Act 2 — the instrument that lied about WHEN, not WHERE.** Getting there required a detective story. Our original perihelion detector kept reporting a wrong number, and the investigation found something subtle and beautiful: Einstein's correction doesn't just swivel Mercury's orbit — it also delays each closest approach by 1.14 seconds per lap. Real physics, his quiet second door. But our detector took its photograph on a fixed schedule, so the delayed run was always photographed a moment earlier in its swing, and that schedule mismatch masqueraded as fake backwards rotation. The cure: teach the detector to interpolate *between* its own ticks and stamp the true instant. The same disease was then found in the Earth-lap counter — at high speed it could only answer in multiples of its own frame time — and the same medicine cured it. Lesson of the week: **an instrument's schedule can contaminate its reading, and the fix is to let the instrument see between its own heartbeats.**

**Act 3 — magnetism closed.** Charged dust now surfs the Sun's magnetic field. The math that turns a particle without ever speeding it up (a magnetic field steers; it never pushes forward) was proven against textbook truth in a standalone lab, then married into the engine. The finale: dust released near the Sun's poles rides a field line, and where the field lines crowd together near the poles, the particle is *reflected* — a magnetic mirror, the same physics that traps particles in Earth's radiation belts. Ours bounce between ±16° latitude on a 160-day shuttle, visibly, on screen.

**Act 4 — light entered.** Sunlight pushes. For small grains, that push competes with the Sun's pull — and because both fade with the square of distance, their *ratio* is a single pure number, beta. Beta one-half is a knife-edge: at 0.49 a grain stays bound; at 0.51 it leaves the solar system forever. At beta exactly 1, push and pull cancel and the grain coasts in a perfect straight line — gravity switched off by light. All of it measured in the lab first, then put on screen: two keys spawn gold "smoke" on either side of the knife-edge, and you watch one orbit while its twin escapes.

**Act 5 — the galaxy arrived.** This is the destination, and it opened with the best mystery in modern astronomy. In the 1970s Vera Rubin measured how fast stars orbit in galaxies and found the outer stars moving *far too fast* — as if most of each galaxy's mass is invisible. We reproduced her discovery at the desk: built the Milky Way's gravity from its visible parts (a dense central bulge plus a flat disk), computed the orbital speeds, and watched them fall off with distance exactly as Newton demands. Then we added an invisible halo of dark matter and the curve went **flat** — outer stars holding ~230 km/s where the visible-only galaxy manages ~117. That gap *is* dark matter, measured as a gravitational effect, which is the only way anyone has ever measured it. Then the mission test was made literal: press `g` and the fabric itself becomes the Milky Way's gravity well, one grid unit per thousand light-years-ish (a kiloparsec), with the monster black hole Sgr A* at center and the Sun's seat marked at its true radius. Press `h` and the dark halo toggles **live**: the outskirts of the well visibly sag and release while the center barely moves — because dark matter's effect lives in the outskirts, exactly where Rubin's curve refused to fall.

### The equations, gently

**The magnetic turn.** A magnetic field bends a charged particle's path without changing its speed — force always at right angles to motion, like a string on a whirling ball. The lab confirmed speed constant to rounding error while the direction wheeled.

**Beta.** Light's push and gravity's pull both weaken with distance squared, so their ratio is one number that never changes with distance: `beta = push / pull`. Below ½ bound, above ½ gone, at 1 a straight line. (Why ½ and not 1? The grains were *born on Earth's orbit*, already moving — they carry Earth's speed, so half the pull is enough to lose them.)

**The rotation curve.** A star's orbital speed obeys `v = √(G·M(<R) / R)` — where `M(<R)` is all the mass *inside* its orbit. If the mass runs out (visible galaxy), speed falls as `1/√R`: Kepler's signature. If speed stays flat, mass must keep growing with radius — mass nobody can see.

**The three-part well.** Bulge (dense ball), disk (flattened, where the Sun lives), halo (invisible, mass growing with radius). Add the speeds in quadrature: `v² = v²_bulge + v²_disk + v²_halo`. The halo term is the difference between a falling curve and a flat one.

### What comes next, in plain words

The well is shaped; next it should be *orbited*. M12c puts real stars on the sheet — a sample from an actual star catalog, each riding the measured rotation curve on the galactic fabric. After that, the deep galaxy backlog: bigger catalogs, a searchable Milky Way, and the black hole at center connected to the horizon math the sim already ships.

## Part II — The Shambu Edition

### The platform, this week's delta

The leapfrog core now carries a **Boris half-turn bracket** — `turn/2, kick, drift, kick, turn/2` — for any body with charge-to-mass `qm`; the bracket restored the DT² convergence law (error ×3.83 on DT halving) and beat the original spec's placement by 2.7×. The unit bridge for magnetism collapsed to one exact constant, `SEC_PER_DAY = 86400`, because rotation needs only an angle. Radiation pressure enters as `applyRadiation` post-pair beside the 1PN hook: acceleration `+beta·G·M☉/r²` outward, no unit bridge at all since beta is dimensionless. `physics.js` now exports `galaxyPhi(R)` — bulge + disk + NFW-style halo in galaxy units (kpc, km/s, M☉; G rebuilt from SI and receipt-checked) — and `fabric.js` has a galaxy mode where **every vertex queries galaxyPhi()** at 1 unit = 1 kpc. The solar sim runs untouched underneath galaxy mode; toggling back heals the sheet next frame. Two keys: `g` galaxy mode, `h` dark halo live.
