## Part I — The Plain-English Edition

### What this project is

fabric-of-space is a solar system — and a galaxy — that lives in a browser. The planets sit where NASA says they actually are, gravity is computed honestly between every pair of bodies, and space itself is drawn as a stretchy grid that sags under mass: the "fabric." One law stands above all others: **the physics never cheats.** Only the picture cheats, to keep things visible, and every visual trick is confessed in a public ledger called CHEATS.md. As of this week the project also has a front door — anyone with a browser can open it.

### What happened this week

Nine milestones in seven days, and they tell one story: the galaxy stopped being a picture and became a measurement.

It opened with **motion**. Four straight spokes of test stars were released into the Milky Way's gravity well and left to run. They wound themselves into spiral arms — not because anyone drew arms, but because the inside of a galaxy turns faster than the outside. Turn the dark matter off and the winding changes character: the outer arms stay stubbornly straight while the inner ones knot up. Dark matter's grip on the outskirts, drawn as a shape.

Then came **the real things**. 145 globular clusters — actual star swarms orbiting the actual Milky Way — were pulled from a public astronomy catalogue, placed at their real distances, and asked a question: *without dark matter, which of you would fly away?* Ten of them. Then the question got sharper. Their sideways motion had never been used, only their motion toward or away from us. This week the sideways part arrived too, measured by the Gaia space telescope, and with the full three-dimensional truth in hand the answer changed: **twenty-one** would escape, not ten. With the dark halo switched on, none do. The invisible thing that holds the Milky Way together was counted twice, by two different methods, and it held.

Midweek the **black hole** was given its actual weight. Sagittarius A*, four million times the Sun, had been a drawn sphere with nothing behind it; now it is in the equations. The check was a star called S2, which loops the black hole every 16.05 years by published measurement. Our machine, given only the published mass and our own gravitational constant, produced 16.08 years. Then the **rotation curve** went on screen — the single most famous graph in this whole subject, the one that made dark matter unavoidable — and it responds live to the halo switch.

Thursday the **disk got a body**: 2,373 real Cepheid stars at real distances and real heights above the galactic plane. They revealed the Milky Way's warp, the way the outer disk bends like a vinyl record left in the sun — one quadrant riding a kilo-parsec high, the opposite one dipping. Nobody drew that. It came out of the data.

Friday the model was finally **put on trial**. 773 of those Cepheids have published circular speeds — how fast they actually go around. They were laid over our model's curve. With dark matter, the model threads them: off by 4.9 km/s. Without it, the model abandons them: off by 55.5. Then, on Saturday, the trial got a verdict *number*. Statisticians have a standard measure of how badly a model misses its data, and it was computed live: **9.0 with dark matter, 1,329.5 without.** One keystroke, and the number leaps by a factor of 148 and turns red.

And on Sunday the doors opened. The whole thing was deployed to a public web address. No install, no download — a link. It carries its own data inside the page, so it works even with the network unplugged.

### The week's honest moment

It did not go perfectly, and the failure is the most instructive thing here. A paste operation quietly deleted 27 lines from the panel whose entire job is proving the data is real. It broke silently and shipped that way to the public URL, where it sat for four commits before anyone pressed the one key that used it.

Two permanent guards were born from that hour. First, an automatic checker that reads every line of code before every deployment and refuses to publish if any name is used that was never defined — the exact shape of the bug. It was proven real by deliberately re-breaking the code and watching it catch the break. Second, a rule about how code is handed over at all: **a paste boundary must always include the line after it**, so nothing below can be swallowed.

Then the incident's deeper lesson was taken seriously. The provenance panel had only ever reported data fetched live. On the public page nothing is fetched — so it reported five absences while 773 real stars sat inside the page. That is technically true and practically a lie. Now every dataset reports the copy actually in the page, with its exact byte count and cryptographic fingerprint, computed fresh in the browser and cross-checked against the same files on disk. Twelve hexadecimal digits, five files, zero disagreement.

### The equations, gently

**Winding.** Everything in a galaxy takes a different time to go around, and the inside is quicker. Start with a straight line of stars and wait: the line has to curl. The arms are not objects, they are a *consequence of the clock running at different speeds at different radii*.

**Escape.** A cluster leaves if it is moving faster than escape speed, and escape speed depends on everything pulling on it. Add the dark halo and escape speed goes up everywhere, so the same cluster at the same speed is suddenly bound. Nobody changed the cluster. The change is in what surrounds it.

**Three dimensions beat one.** A speed measured only along the line of sight is a shadow of the real speed. This week the shadow was replaced with the object — and the count of escapees doubled. A lesson learned the hard way earlier in the project got its final proof here: the shadow can be *longer* than the object, because the Sun is moving too, and its motion is baked into what we see.

**Chi-square, in one sentence.** Take each measured point, ask how far the model missed, divide that miss by how well the point is known, square it, and average. About 1 means the model is as good as the measurement can tell. 9 means a decent toy. 1,329 means annihilation.

### What comes next, in plain words

The verdict panel is a scoreboard. The next step makes it a **scale**: a knob for the dark halo's mass, turned live, watching the verdict number slide down into a valley and back up. The bottom of that valley is a measurement of how much dark matter the Milky Way contains — made by hand, in a browser, from real stars. That is the galaxy-tier answer to Mercury's 42.8 arcseconds.

### The two that were physics, not plumbing

**M12e, the frame lesson.** Switching from Vlsr to full 3D doubled the escapee count because a line-of-sight speed is a projection, and the projection carries the Sun's own 232 km/s. `|Vlsr|` is not a floor on true galactocentric speed — NGC 3201 reads 481.9 by line of sight and 367.6 in truth. The W29-era "floor" claim was invalidated and filed in CHEATS.

**M12j, the acquittal.** MZ8 is the deepest receipt the project has produced. A fake sky drawn from the halo-ON model plus noise at exactly the measurement scale must read χ²/ν near **1, not 0** — a true model matches its data to within the error bars and no better. It read 1.00. The same fake sky still convicts the halo-OFF model at 1357.8, within 2% of the real sky's 1329.5, because the halo gap dwarfs both the noise and the model's own 4.9 km/s error. A statistic that survives having its data swapped for a simulation is testing the *model*.

### Bug ledger — three new entries

**#7, the bracket bluff (M12f).** A bisection shipped with `hi = 0.05`, sized only for the real black hole. At 100× mass the true root (101.9 pc) escapes the bracket and the bisection returned **its own edge** with a straight face — 103.8% error, fail-silent. Caught by Shambu's negative test; he diagnosed the widening himself before the verdict came back.

**#8, the no-op negative (M12g).** The planned sabotage — swap `log10` for `ln` — mutates nothing, because swapped consistently the bases cancel in the ratio. A negative test must be *verified to change an observable*. The truer failure (reading a log axis as linear) was caught at 25.9%.

**#9, the edge anchor (A1).** A paste boundary placed at the opening line of the following function. Nothing in the replacement proved the following lines survived, and 27 of them did not. Fail-silent until the one key that used them was pressed — four commits and one public deployment later. Rule earned: **paste anchors sandwich, never edge.**

### Two errata, both self-caught

The W29 recap placed the 88.4 km/s dark-matter gap at the Sun's seat. `galaxyLab` R3 always measured it at **24.6 kpc**; the seat's own gap is **47.1**. Corrected in commit `ab8a05f` with an erratum footer. The error was the recap author's; the lab was right throughout — caught while designing V0. Receipts audit their own paperwork.

The R1 FAIL-before was captured only after the fix had landed, against a scratch copy of `physics.js` recovered from `fb54627`. A negative test never seen to fail is theater; it was made to fail before the receipt was accepted.

### Operational footnote

Track A opened Sunday on an honest audit: the physics was strong, the packaging was at zero. Three walls — nobody can run it, nobody knows what to press, half the instrument lives in the console. A1 took the first. The deploy gate now runs `eslint no-undef` across 27 files before every build, and the bundle carries its data via `?raw` imports, so the hosted page boots with no network at all.

The week's last hours spilled into Monday: A1.1 (compiled-snapshot provenance, `.gitattributes` pinning `data/**` to LF so a checksum cannot depend on the operating system) and A2 (the `?` legend generated from a single 25-key array, guarded by `legendLab`, which fails the deploy if the docs and the code disagree). Both belong to W31 and are logged in HANDOFF sessions 13 and 14.
