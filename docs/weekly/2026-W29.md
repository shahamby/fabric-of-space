## Part I — The Plain-English Edition

### What this project is

fabric-of-space is a solar system that lives in a browser — and, as of this week, a galaxy too. The planets sit where NASA says they actually are, gravity is computed honestly between every pair of bodies, and space itself is drawn as a stretchy grid that sags under mass — the "fabric." One law stands above all others: **the physics never cheats.** Only the picture cheats, to keep things visible, and every visual trick is confessed in a public ledger called CHEATS.md. Nine confessions by week's end, each documented like an incident report.

### What happened this week

Three acts. In the first, the measurement promised last week landed: two identical universes were booted from the same sky, one with Einstein's correction and one without, and everything the two shared was subtracted away. What refused to cancel was **+42.8 arcseconds per century** by one witness and **+42.9** by a second, independent one — against Einstein's predicted 42.98. The dime on the wall 85 meters away, measured in a browser. Then came the detective story: our older instrument had been reporting a strange −91, and the verdict is beautiful. Einstein's correction doesn't just swivel Mercury's orbit — it *retimes* it, delaying every closest approach by 1.14 seconds per lap. A clock that only reads on the simulation's step grid saw that slow slide as fake backwards motion. The cure: teach the clock to read *between* its own ticks. Both witnesses now agree.

In the second act, two new forces entered without disturbing the first. Magnetism: charged dust now turns under the Sun's magnetic field using a second integrator built beside the trusted one, and polar grains bounce between mirror points at ±16° latitude like particles trapped in Earth's radiation belts. Light: sunlight now *pushes*, controlled by one dimensionless dial called beta — the push-to-pull ratio. At beta one-half sits a knife-edge: 0.49 stays bound, 0.51 leaves forever. At beta exactly one, gravity and light cancel and a grain coasts in a perfect straight line — a check Shambu wrote by hand, and it drifted by exactly zero.

The third act is the destination arriving. The Milky Way was **measured before it was rendered**: a three-part model (dense bulge, flat disk, invisible dark halo) reproduced the Sun's 232 km/s orbit, and turning the halo off opened an 88.4 km/s gap — dark matter, not as a slogan but as a number. Then the well went on screen: press one key and the fabric re-shapes into the galaxy's potential, one grid unit per kiloparsec, with a live switch that removes the dark halo and visibly relaxes the rim. Then the well was *orbited*: four straight spokes of test stars wound themselves into spiral arms, lagging harder in the outskirts without the halo's grip. And at week's close, 147 real globular clusters — with real measured speeds — were fetched from a public astronomy archive, checked against the catalogue's own published numbers, and staged for the question: without dark matter, which of these would fly away?

### The equations, gently

**The subtraction.** Two universes sharing the same start, the same steps, the same everything except one correction. Subtract their readings lap by lap and every shared flaw cancels. Only the correction survives. That's the whole measurement design.

**The clock between ticks.** Any meter that reads only when the simulation happens to step can hallucinate motion that isn't there. The fix is to notice the true moment always falls *between* two steps, and slide the reading to it with a small curve fit. Two instruments were cured with the same medicine this week.

**The half-turn sandwich.** A magnetic field turns a moving charge but never speeds it up. So the turn is wrapped *around* the gravity kick — half a turn, kick, glide, kick, half a turn — and speed stays exact by construction.

**Push over pull.** Sunlight's push and gravity's pull both fade with the square of distance, so their ratio is one pure number, the same everywhere. Half means knife-edge. One means straight-line coasting, as if the Sun weren't there at all.

**The flat curve.** Planets obey Kepler: farther means slower. The galaxy refuses — the rotation curve stays flat far past where visible matter runs out. The refusal is dark matter. Here it measures 88.4 km/s at the Sun's seat.

**Staying versus leaving.** Every point in the well has an escape speed set by the well's depth there. Ten real clusters move faster than the visible-matter well can hold — and slower than the well-plus-halo can. Dark matter is the difference between "bound" and "gone."

### What comes next, in plain words

First, the clusters go on screen (that push landed Monday). Then three doors: give the clusters their sideways motion from the Gaia space telescope so they trace real orbits instead of sitting still; give the central black hole its true four-million-Sun mass so the innermost curve turns properly Keplerian; or give the flat disk its thickness so stars bob through the plane. The gravity engine stays honest through all of it.

## Part II — The Shambu Edition

### The platform, in one paragraph

Two unit systems now share one codebase and never mix. The solar core is untouched: barycentric ecliptic J2000, AU/day/M☉, leapfrog with the Boris half-turn bracket, the 1PN term behind its toggle, and the radiation hook — all instruments sealed to `E0`. Galaxy mode runs in kpc, km/s, and Myr with `G = 4.301 × 10⁻⁶ kpc·(km/s)²·M☉⁻¹`, rebuilt from SI and receipted (the W0 bridge check opens every galaxy lab). The deeper change is procedural: **every milestone now opens in `lab/`** — dependency-free node scripts with sealed predictions stated before running, PASS/FAIL receipts logged before any browser wiring. Determinism ceremonies (two boots, different `timeScale`, identical readings) are standard practice, not events.

### The standing equations, new arrivals

The Boris bracket, married into `leapfrogStep` without divorcing it:

```
turn(dt/2) · kick(dt/2) · drift(dt) · kick(dt/2) · turn(dt/2)
```

Speed is invariant by construction (rotation does no work); the bracket placement restored the DT² law, ×3.83 error reduction on halving.

Radiation pressure as a pure number:

```
a_rad = β · G·M☉/r²  (outward)   →   net pull = (1 − β) · gravity
```

Both sides scale as 1/r², so β needs no unit bridge. Knife-edge at β = ½ (0.49 bound with apoapsis 50.00 AU; 0.51 escapes). β = 1 coasts straight — W4's drift measured exactly 0.

The galaxy's three parts, as the lab spends them (kpc, km/s, M☉):

```
v²_bulge = G·M_b·R / (R + a_b)²                      M_b 1.5e10, a_b 0.5
v²_disk  = G·M_d·R² / (R² + (a_d + b_d)²)^1.5        M_d 6.5e10, a_d 3.0, b_d 0.3
v²_halo  = G·M_s·[ln(1+x) − x/(1+x)] / R,  x = R/R_s  M_s 5.0e11, R_s 16
```

Sun's seat at 8.2 kpc: 232.1 km/s with the halo, an 88.4 km/s gap without it. Escape speed follows from depth, `v_esc = √(2·|Φ(R)|)` — the number the cluster census interrogates.

### Bug ledger addition

**Pattern #11: invisible does not mean unpickable.** Three.js `intersectObjects` tests layers, not `.visible`. In galaxy mode every solar body mesh is hidden but still answers raycasts, and the Sun's display sphere sits *inside* Sgr A*'s drawn sphere at the origin — so clicking the black hole reported "Sun," and the stale panel rode the mode switch showing AU in a kpc scene. Cure: filter pick targets by `.visible`, swap target lists on mode change, clear selection on `g`.
